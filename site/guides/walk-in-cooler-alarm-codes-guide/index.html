Walk-In Cooler Alarm Codes: First, Figure Out Which Controller Is Talking

Here's the thing that trips people up when a walk-in starts alarming: the brand on the door — Norlake, Kolpak, or a local box builder — usually isn't the brand doing the talking. The box is insulation and panels. The alarms come from the refrigeration system and its controller, and a handful of controller families cover most of the commercial market. Identify which one you're looking at, and the code list follows.

Heatcraft intelliGen — AL, ER, and IN codes

Heatcraft (whose equipment sells under the Bohn and Larkin names) ships the intelliGen controller on current unit coolers: a small OLED display with a colored light bar. Red bar plus an AL code is an alarm — AL01 is high box temperature, AL06 the door alarm. Yellow plus an ER code is an error, usually a sensor — ER01 is the box temperature sensor. IN codes are informational. Newer A2L-refrigerant units add leak-detection alarms (AL12, ER32) that you should treat as evacuate-and-call events. Full Heatcraft list →

Heatcraft Beacon II — A and E codes

The previous generation of Heatcraft smart systems shows A1-A4 alarms and E1-E9 errors on the board display, with an optional Smart Controller LCD that flashes text like *BOXHI and +COMM+. The logic mirrors intelliGen: A1 high box temp, A4/E-codes for failed sensors — several of which pump the system down and keep it off until repaired, which makes them urgent. Full Beacon II list →

KE2 Therm — named, scrolling alarms

KE2 Evaporator Efficiency controllers don't use cryptic codes at all — they scroll names: HIGH AIR TEMP, DOOR SWITCH, EXCESS DEFROST, LOW SUPERHEAT. Yellow LED means the system is still running; a red-LED alarm like PRESSURE SENSOR means it shut down to protect the compressor and the clock is running on your product. Full KE2 list →

Dixell — the two-letter codes on Norlake, Kolpak and many others

The little Dixell (Emerson) XR-series thermostat is everywhere in walk-ins. Its codes are terse: P1-P4 are probe failures, HA/LA high and low temperature alarms, dA door alarm, EA/CA external alarms — with CA being the serious one that switches everything off. And the one that generates the most confused phone calls isn't a fault at all: OF/PoF just means the keypad is locked (hold up and down arrows for 3 seconds). Full Dixell list →

Norlake control panels — plain-text alarms

Norlake's programmable walk-in panels (built on Carel hardware) skip codes entirely and scroll plain text: DOOR AJAR ALARM, ROOM TEMP HIGH ALARM, AIR FLOW LOSS. Read them literally, silence with the ALARM button while you investigate, and check the status line for fallback modes like SENSOR FAIL MODE that mean the panel is limping on backup logic. Full Norlake list →

The universal rules, whatever the controller

  1. Photograph the display before resetting anything. The code plus the box temperature is your service call in one picture.
  2. Know whether the system is still running. A running system with a high-temp alarm is urgency measured in hours; a shut-down system is urgency measured in product.
  3. Log box temperature every 30 minutes during any event — for food safety compliance and for your own diagnosis.
  4. One reset is diagnosis. An alarm that returns is telling the truth; forcing restarts against a pressure or superheat fault is how compressors die.
