Why is my walk-in cooler not cooling or holding temperature?

A warm walk-in is a race against your inventory, so this list is ordered by speed: the fastest, cheapest checks first. Keep the door shut while you work through it — a full, closed box holds temperature for hours; an open one doesn't.

1. Check the controller for an alarm code — 30 seconds

If your box has a digital controller, it has probably already diagnosed itself. Heatcraft's intelliGen shows AL and ER codes, a Beacon II system shows A and E codes, KE2 Therm controllers scroll named alarms, and Dixell controllers on Norlake and Kolpak boxes show two-letter codes like HA (high temperature) or P1 (probe failure). Look the exact code up on this site before touching anything — it tells you whether the system is still trying to cool or has shut itself down, which changes everything about urgency.

2. The door — the number one cause, and it's free

A door propped open during a delivery, a torn or hard gasket, a sagging hinge, a latch that doesn't pull tight, a strip curtain that got tied back and never released — door problems cause more warm walk-ins than every mechanical failure combined. Check that the door actually seals: a dollar bill closed in the gasket should drag when you pull it. While you're there, check the door switch; a misaligned switch tells the controller the door is open and can shut fans down.

3. The evaporator coil — is it a block of ice?

Look at the coil unit inside the box. If it's buried in ice, air can't move through it and the box can't cool, no matter how healthy the refrigeration system is. Icing always has a cause — a failed defrost heater, a defrost schedule problem, a door leaking humid air, or a failed coil probe ending defrosts wrong — and melting it without fixing the cause buys you days, not a repair. Note it for the tech; don't chip at it.

4. The evaporator fans

Fans should run whenever the box is calling for cooling (some configurations stop them when the door opens). Fan blades not turning means no air across the coil — check the breaker, then look for ice binding the blades. Motor and contactor replacement is a service job.

5. The condensing unit — where the heat actually goes

Find the condensing unit (on the roof, out back, or on top of the box). Is it running? Is the coil clean, or matted with dust, lint, and grease? Is airflow blocked by stored boxes, a fence, or leaves? A dirty or suffocated condenser can't reject the box's heat — in summer it trips high-pressure safeties, and on Dixell-equipped units you may see the HA2 condenser alarm. Check its breaker too. Cleaning a condenser coil (power off first) is legitimate in-house maintenance.

6. What changed today?

Warm product loaded straight into the box, a big delivery with the door open twenty minutes, a deep-cleaning crew that unplugged something, a breaker tripped during a storm. Half of "broken" walk-ins are a normal box digesting an abnormal day. Give a loaded box time to recover — but log the temperature every 30 minutes while you wait, both for food safety and so you know if it's actually recovering.

When it's a real service call

Door sealing, coil clear, fans running, condenser clean and running, breakers on — and it's still warm? What's left is refrigerant charge, valves, and the compressor: licensed-technician territory, and worth calling fast because product loss usually costs more than the visit. Tell them the controller code, the box temperature and trend, and what you've already checked — that's the difference between one truck roll and two.
