Ice Buildup in Your Walk-In: It's a Symptom, Not the Disease

Ice in a walk-in freezer looks like a nuisance you chip away at. It's actually the box keeping a written record of a problem: every pound of ice is moisture that got in and a defrost system that couldn't keep up. Where the ice forms tells you what's wrong.

Ice on the evaporator coil — defrost isn't finishing its job

The evaporator naturally frosts during operation; the defrost cycle (electric heaters in a freezer) melts it off several times a day. A coil turning into a glacier means defrost is failing: burned-out heater elements, a failed defrost termination sensor ending cycles too early, a timer or controller problem, or simply not enough defrost cycles for how much moisture the box is taking in. Controllers document this — Heatcraft's intelliGen flags coil-sensor errors like ER02, Dixell's P2 probe failure makes defrosts end on time instead of temperature, and KE2 controllers log DEFR TERM ON TIME when two defrosts in a row can't reach termination temperature. An iced coil also blocks airflow — which is how an ice problem becomes a warm-box problem, and eventually a low-superheat condition that can flood liquid refrigerant back to the compressor. That's the expensive ending this article exists to prevent.

Ice around the door — the box is breathing humid air

Ice on the door frame, the ceiling just inside the door, or the floor at the threshold means outside air is getting in and dumping its moisture: a torn or hardened gasket, a door that doesn't self-close and latch, a failed frame heater on the freezer door letting the gasket freeze to the frame (and then get torn by the next person yanking it open), or simply a door that stands open through every delivery. Fixes here are cheap — gaskets, sweeps, door closers, strip curtains, staff habits — and they're the highest-return maintenance a walk-in gets, because every one of them also cuts the load on defrost and the compressor.

Ice under the coil or on the floor — the drain line froze

Defrost water has to leave the box through a drain line. In a freezer, that line is supposed to be heated and pitched; when the drain heater fails or the pitch is wrong, meltwater refreezes in the pan or the line, then overflows as sheet ice under the evaporator and puddles that become a skating rink. Floor ice is a slip injury and a health-inspection finding waiting to happen — treat a frozen drain as a real repair, not a mop-and-forget.

What staff can safely do

  • Fix the moisture sources: gaskets, closers, curtains, door discipline during deliveries.
  • Keep product from blocking the evaporator's airflow — cartons stacked into the air stream cause localized icing.
  • Log when and where ice reappears after a defrost — that pattern is diagnostic gold for the tech.
  • Don't chip ice off the coil with tools. Fins bend, tubes puncture, and a punctured evaporator turns a service call into a replacement.

When to call

Any recurring coil icing, any suspected heater or sensor failure, and any frozen drain: these involve live electrical heaters, refrigeration diagnosis, and controller settings — technician work.
